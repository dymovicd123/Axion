Infinite limits

What happens when there is no finite value?

So far, our functions have approached numbers like 4 or 10. But a function can also grow without bound. Its values might become larger and larger, or more and more negative, as x approaches a point.

Look at f(x) = 1 / (x − 2). When x gets close to 2, the denominator becomes a very small number. Dividing 1 by a tiny number gives a result with a large magnitude.

For example, when x = 1.9, f(x) = −10. When x = 1.99, f(x) = −100. On the other side, x = 2.01 gives 100.

Why does the side matter?

To the left of 2, x − 2 is negative. The values fall toward negative infinity. To the right, x − 2 is positive, and the values rise toward positive infinity.

x → 2⁻ : f(x) → −∞

x → 2⁺ : f(x) → +∞

Because the two sides behave differently, the two-sided limit at x = 2 does not exist. Also, infinity is not an ordinary number that the function reaches. The notation describes how the values grow.

Vertical asymptotes

The vertical line x = 2 is called a vertical asymptote. The graph gets closer to this line while rising or falling sharply.

Not all infinite limits behave in opposite directions. For example, 1 / (x − 2)² is positive on both sides of 2. Its values grow toward +∞ from either side.

When solving these problems, pay attention to whether the denominator is positive or negative as you approach the point.
