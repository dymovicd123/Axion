Introduction to integrals

Sometimes we know how quickly something is changing, but we want to know the total amount that has accumulated. Integrals help us find that total.

Think about filling a tank

Water flows into a tank. If the flow rate stays at 3 litres per second, the tank receives 3 litres in one second, 6 litres in two seconds, and 12 litres in four seconds.

This is just multiplication: volume = rate × time. But what happens if the flow rate changes while the tank is filling?

Adding up small amounts

Imagine dividing the time into many short intervals. For each interval, we estimate how much water entered, then add all those amounts together. The smaller the intervals, the more accurate the estimate.

A definite integral describes that total:

Total water = ∫₀ᵀ r(t) dt

Here, r(t) is the flow rate in litres per second, and T is the time in seconds. The result is in litres.

An increasing flow rate

Suppose the flow rate follows r(t) = 1 + t. It starts at 1 L/s and increases by 1 L/s every second.

The amount of water added from time 0 to time T is:

V(T) = ∫₀ᵀ (1 + t) dt = T + T²/2

After 4 seconds, the current flow rate is 5 L/s, but the total volume added is 12 litres. Again, the rate and the accumulated amount are not the same thing.
