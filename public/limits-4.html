Infinity and asymptotes

Distinguish unbounded behaviour near a point from behaviour far away, calculate limits of rational functions and design a curve with prescribed asymptotes.

1. Unbounded behaviour near a point

A function can grow beyond every positive bound as x approaches a number. We write +∞ for this behaviour and −∞ for unbounded negative values. Infinity is not a real number, and an infinite limit is not a finite limit.

Worked example

For 1/(x − 2), x→2⁻ gives negative denominators approaching zero, so the outputs tend to −∞. From the right they tend to +∞. There is a vertical asymptote x = 2, but no common two-sided limit, even in the extended sense.

In the first column of outputs, a negative denominator gives a negative result and a positive denominator gives a positive result. In the squared case, both denominators are positive, so both sides increase without bound. The line x = 2 is a vertical asymptote because at least one side becomes unbounded. A denominator equal to zero alone is not enough: a common factor may instead create a removable hole.

2. Limits at infinity

In x→∞, the input itself grows without bound. A horizontal asymptote y = L describes a finite limiting height as x moves far to the right or left. This is different from a vertical asymptote, which concerns a finite input.

Consider 3 + 2/x. The term 2/x approaches zero as x grows in either direction. The graph can approach 3 from above or below without being equal to 3 at any finite input.

Worked example

limx→∞ (3 + 2/x) = 3

At x = 10, the error is 0.2. At x = 100, it is 0.02. A tenfold increase in x reduces this error by a factor of ten.

Near a point versus far away

In x → 2 the input stays near 2, but the output might become infinitely large. In x → +∞ the input itself becomes arbitrarily large. In x → −∞ the input becomes arbitrarily negative. The output may still approach an ordinary number in either case.

Example: approach from below

For f(x) = 7 − 3/x, positive x gives an output below 7. At x = 10, f(x) = 6.7; at 100, f(x) = 6.97. The absolute error is |f(x) − 7| = 3/x for x > 0. Thus the horizontal asymptote is y = 7.

To guarantee an error below 0.01 for every sufficiently large positive x, solve 3/x < 0.01. Multiplying by x > 0 gives x > 300. The threshold is 300.

3. Rational functions and leading powers

For a rational function P(x)/Q(x), divide numerator and denominator by the largest power of x in the denominator. If the numerator degree is smaller, the limit is zero. If the degrees are equal, the limit is the ratio of leading coefficients. If the numerator degree is greater, the quotient is unbounded in magnitude; the sign depends on the degree difference, direction and leading coefficients.

Worked example

(6x² − x + 1)/(2x² + 4) = (6 − 1/x + 1/x²)/(2 + 4/x²) → 3

The same finite limit holds as x→−∞. Lower powers vanish after division. This is about large inputs, not substituting infinity as if it were a number.

Why the largest powers matter

For large |x|, 1/x and 1/x² tend to zero. Dividing all terms by the denominator's highest power makes these smaller terms visible. Here are three contrasting examples as x → +∞:

In the last example the limit toward negative infinity is −∞ instead. When the numerator degree is greater, check the leading term and direction rather than assuming the limit is always +∞.

4. Design a curve from its asymptotes

A family of curves f(x) = A/(x − h) + k has a vertical asymptote x = h and horizontal asymptote y = k when A ≠ 0. The sign of A determines which way the branches diverge. Changing A also changes the rate of approach to the horizontal asymptote.

Design a model from its properties rather than copying a predefined formula. The graph separates the branches at the vertical asymptote. A valid model need not match a single reference answer.

Example: construct a different curve

Suppose we want a vertical asymptote x = 1, a horizontal asymptote y = 2 and a point (3, 5). Start with f(x) = A/(x − h) + k. Choose h = 1 and k = 2 to set the asymptotes. Substituting (3, 5) gives 5 = A/2 + 2, so A = 6.

Because A is positive, the right branch tends to +∞ near x = 1. The resulting equation is f(x) = 6/(x − 1) + 2. A range of allowed point heights instead of an exact point would allow several choices of A.
