Calculating limits

Choose a valid algebraic method, simplify expressions without changing the nearby function, and distinguish an indeterminate form from a limit.

1. Substitution and limit laws

If f and g approach finite limits A and B, their sum approaches A + B and their product approaches AB. A quotient approaches A/B provided B ≠ 0. Polynomials are continuous everywhere, so evaluating a polynomial limit usually means direct substitution. Rational functions are continuous wherever their denominator is nonzero.

Worked example

limx→2 (x² + 3)/(x + 1) = (4 + 3)/3 = 7/3

The denominator tends to 3, so the quotient law applies. There is no reason to factor or rationalise.

Which operation is allowed?

ExpressionLimit if f → A and g → B
f + gA + B
f − gA − B
fgAB
f/gA/B, only if B ≠ 0

Start by substituting the target into the numerator and denominator separately. For example, in (x² − 4)/(x + 3) as x → 1, the numerator approaches −3 and the denominator approaches 4. Since 4 is not zero, the limit is −3/4. We did not need to change the expression.

2. The indeterminate form 0/0

Substitution sometimes produces 0/0. This is not a number and does not specify the limit. For example, x/x tends to 1 at zero while x²/x tends to 0; both initially give 0/0. Simplify the expression for nearby nonzero denominators, then evaluate the resulting limit.

Worked example

(x² − 5x + 6)/(x − 2) = (x − 2)(x − 3)/(x − 2) = x − 3, x ≠ 2

The limit at 2 is −1. The condition x ≠ 2 is essential: the original expression is undefined there.

Why we can cancel here

If substitution gives 0/0, first ask whether numerator and denominator share a factor. In a quadratic x² + bx + c, find two numbers whose sum is b and whose product is c. In x² − 5x + 6 these are −2 and −3.

(x − 2)(x − 3)/(x − 2) = x − 3   (x ≠ 2)

We are allowed to cancel x − 2 for nearby x because those inputs are not 2. Taking the limit of x − 3 then gives −1. The original function has a hole at 2, so its value at 2 remains undefined.

Do not cancel terms in a sum: (x + 2)/x does not simplify to 2. Only a common multiplicative factor can be cancelled. The form 0/0 alone does not tell us that the limit exists.

3. Rationalising a square root

When a difference of square roots tends to zero, multiplying by its conjugate can remove the troublesome difference. The identity (u − v)(u + v) = u² − v² makes this work. Multiplying both numerator and denominator by the same nonzero expression preserves the quotient.

Worked example

(√(x + 9) − 3)/x = (x + 9 − 9)/(x(√(x + 9) + 3))
= 1/(√(x + 9) + 3), x ≠ 0

The limit at zero is 1/6. The conjugate tends to 6 and is nonzero near zero.

What is a conjugate?

For a difference u − v, its conjugate is u + v. Their product is u² − v², because the middle terms cancel. For example, (√(x + 1) − 1)(√(x + 1) + 1) = x.

In the worked example above, direct substitution makes both the numerator and denominator zero. Multiplying by the conjugate divided by itself changes neither the quotient nor its nearby values. The numerator becomes x, which cancels with the denominator's x for x ≠ 0. Now the denominator approaches 3 + 3 = 6, so substitution is safe and gives 1/6.

Notice that a conjugate is an expression, not a numerical answer until an input is chosen. The next exercise asks for its value at the target as well as the final limit.

4. Choose the method before calculating

Start with substitution. If the denominator is nonzero, use the quotient law. For 0/0, look for a common factor, a conjugate, or a common denominator. If only the denominator tends to zero, cancellation is not automatically possible; the function may grow without bound, a topic in the next lesson.

Worked example

(1/(x + 1) − 1)/x = (1 − (x + 1))/(x(x + 1)) = −1/(x + 1)

This limit at zero equals −1. Here a common denominator is more useful than factoring a quadratic.

Fractions within a fraction

When two fractions are subtracted, use a common denominator before attempting cancellation. For example, 1/(x + 1) − 1 = [1 − (x + 1)]/(x + 1) = −x/(x + 1). Dividing this by x gives −1/(x + 1), provided x ≠ 0 and x ≠ −1. Substitution at zero now gives −1.

Choosing a method

  • Nonzero denominator after substitution: substitute directly.
  • 0/0 with polynomial factors: factor and cancel.
  • 0/0 with a difference of square roots: try the conjugate.
  • 0/0 with a difference of fractions: combine the fractions first.
  • Nonzero numerator over a denominator approaching zero: examine signs on both sides instead of cancelling.
