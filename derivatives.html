Introduction to derivatives

A derivative tells us how quickly something changes at a particular moment. A useful example is the speed of a moving car.

Average change and instant change

Suppose you drive 20 metres in 5 seconds. Your average speed is 4 metres per second. But you might have been slower at the start and faster near the end. Average speed cannot tell us your exact speed at one moment.

A derivative is a way to find that instantaneous rate of change. On a distance–time graph, it is the slope of the tangent line at a point.

A simple example

Let the car's distance from its starting point be s(t) = t², with time in seconds and distance in metres. After 2 seconds the car is 4 metres from the start; after 3 seconds it is 9 metres away.

The derivative of t² is 2t. This gives us the car's speed:

s(t) = t²   →   v(t) = 2t

At 3 seconds, its instantaneous speed is 6 m/s. The car has travelled 9 metres, but its speed is not 9 m/s. Distance and speed are different quantities.

Where does 2t come from?

We start with the average change over a very small time interval h:

[s(t + h) − s(t)] / h = [(t + h)² − t²] / h = 2t + h

As h approaches 0, the extra h disappears and we get v(t) = 2t. That is the main idea behind the derivative.
